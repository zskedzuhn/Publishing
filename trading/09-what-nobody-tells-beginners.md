# 09 — What Nobody Tells Beginners

The previous eight chapters are the curriculum. This one is the conversation you
would have with someone who has been doing this a while — the things that do not
fit into a framework but that determine how your first year actually goes.

Read it after you have read the rest. It will mean more.

---

## Your first year has one job

Not profit. **Survival and data.**

You are trying to still be here in twelve months with a log of 200+ trades and
an honest picture of what you do well and badly. That is the entire objective.
A trader who ends year one down 8% with a clean journal and a documented edge is
in vastly better shape than one who is up 30% from three lucky oversized trades
and cannot tell you why.

The second trader will give it all back. They have learned that oversizing works.

Judge year one on: did I follow my rules, did I keep records, am I still
solvent. Nothing else.

---

## Screen time is not practice

The Brazilian study found no evidence of learning through experience among
traders who persisted more than 300 days. Sit with that, because it contradicts
the most common advice you will receive.

Hours in front of a chart do not compound into skill. **Structured feedback
does.** Ten trades that you logged, graded, and reviewed teach you more than two
hundred you took and forgot. The people who tell you to "get screen time" are
describing what they did, not what worked.

Practice means: a written hypothesis, an executed plan, a recorded outcome, and
a review that separates decision quality from result. Everything else is
watching.

---

## Trade one thing until you know it

Beginners scan forty tickers looking for setups. This is backwards.

Pick one instrument. SPY, or ES futures, or one liquid large-cap. Trade only that
for six months. You are learning its personality — how it behaves at the open,
how far it typically extends, what a real breakout looks like in that name versus
a fake one, how it acts on a Fed day.

That knowledge is not transferable and it is not available from a book. It is
also the closest thing to an edge a small trader can build, because the
institutions are not paying attention to whether SPY tends to fade the first
fifteen-minute push on quiet Tuesdays. You can be.

Forty tickers means you know none of them.

---

## Most of your profit comes from very few trades

Look at any profitable trader's distribution and you find the same shape: a
majority of trades are small wins, small losses, and scratches, and the year is
made by a handful of outliers that ran.

This has a specific practical consequence. **The habit of taking profit early
does not cost you a little — it removes your entire year.** If you cut every
winner at +1R because green feels good, you never capture the +6R trade that
pays for the forty losers around it.

Letting a winner run is far harder than cutting a loser. Cutting a loser is a
rule you can automate with a bracket order. Letting a winner run requires you to
sit through unrealized profit going backwards, repeatedly, and not touch it. This
is the skill that takes longest to build, and almost nobody warns you about it
because it sounds like a good problem to have.

Partial exits are the honest compromise: take half at 2R, trail the rest. You
give up some upside for the psychological ability to actually hold something.

---

## No-trade days are wins

The market does not owe you a setup today. Some days there is nothing.

A day where you sat, watched, saw nothing that met your criteria, and closed the
platform having done nothing is a **successful day**. You correctly identified
the absence of opportunity. That is a skill, and it is worth money.

Beginners cannot do this. They feel that not trading is not working, so they
manufacture a trade out of something that half-resembles their setup. Then they
pay the spread, the commission, and usually the stop.

The urge to trade is strongest exactly when there is nothing to trade — because
in a good market you are already positioned. Boredom, not fear, is what empties
most small accounts.

---

## Sizing down always works and is always available

Whatever is wrong, smaller size makes it better.

In a drawdown and second-guessing yourself? Cut size in half. Your losses shrink,
the emotional load drops, and you keep gathering data. Coming back after time
away? Start at a quarter size. Testing a new setup? Minimum size until it has 30
trades behind it.

There is no downside to this. The only cost is slower profit, and you were not
making profit anyway — that is why you are sizing down. Meanwhile it buys the one
thing you actually need, which is more trades before you run out of money.

Nobody ever blew up an account by trading too small. Every blown account has
oversizing somewhere in its history.

---

## You will not recognize yourself in a drawdown

Read your own rules now, while you are calm. They seem obvious. Of course you
would not move a stop. Of course you would honor the daily loss limit.

Then you are down four days running, you are questioning whether any of this
works, and a trade goes against you. In that moment you will generate a
completely reasonable-sounding argument for why this specific situation is an
exception. It will not feel like rule-breaking. It will feel like judgment.

This is why the rules have to be mechanical and set in advance. Not because you
lack discipline, but because **the version of you who wrote the rules and the
version who breaks them are not the same person, and the second one is more
persuasive in the moment.**

Bracket orders, hard daily limits, and a written plan are not bureaucracy. They
are the calm version of you constraining the panicked version, which is the only
protection that has ever worked.

---

## Have income from somewhere else

The traders who last have rent covered by something other than trading.

This is not a lifestyle preference, it is a performance factor. When your P&L has
to produce this month's rent, you cannot take a no-trade day, you cannot size
down, and you cannot sit through a drawdown. You are forced into exactly the
behaviors that lose money. Financial pressure to perform reliably causes
underperformance.

The corollary is worth stating plainly: **quitting your job to day trade is close
to the worst possible sequencing.** You have simultaneously removed your income
and added pressure to a fragile skill. The Brazilian paper is titled *Day Trading
for a Living?* and the finding is that essentially nobody does.

Trade alongside income. If it works, the account will grow and eventually the
question answers itself. If it does not, you still have a life.

---

## The good ones are boring

The cultural image of a trader is fast decisions and adrenaline. The reality of
people who do this sustainably is closer to a bookkeeper.

Same setup, same instrument, same window, same size, logged, reviewed, day after
day. Three trades and done. Closes the laptop at 11am. Reviews on Sunday. It
looks tedious from outside, and that is the point — the boredom is the edge.
Excitement in trading is a symptom of oversizing or gambling, not of skill.

If your trading is thrilling, something is wrong with your position sizing.

---

## Your edge will decay

Whatever works now will work less well later. Markets adapt, volatility regimes
change, and the specific behavior you learned to exploit gets crowded or
disappears.

This is normal and it means the job is never "find a strategy" — it is *keep
finding strategies.* The journal is what tells you when the decay started,
because you will not feel it. You will just have a run of losses that looks like
every other run of losses, and only the data separates "normal variance" from
"this stopped working three months ago."

Traders who stop reviewing get killed by this, usually slowly.

---

## Nobody is coming to help you

There is no mentor who will hand you an edge, no signal service that survives
contact with reality, no chatroom where the profitable traders share their real
positions in advance. The people offering these things make money from the
offering, not from the trading.

You will build your edge yourself out of your own logged observations of one
instrument, or you will not have one. That is unglamorous and it is also the
actual answer.

---

## The specific tells that you are in trouble

Watch for these in yourself. Each one is a reliable predictor of a bad stretch:

- You are checking positions on your phone constantly.
- You are trading instruments you did not trade last month.
- You know your P&L to the dollar at all times but have not updated your journal
  in a week.
- You have started reading about a position after entering it.
- You are calculating what you need to make to get back to even.
- You have moved a stop, once, and it worked out.
- You are trading at 2pm when your window ends at 11am.
- You feel you *deserve* a winning trade.

Any two of these together: cut size to a quarter and go review your log. Do not
try to trade your way out of it. Nobody has ever succeeded at that.

---

## The equity curve is the only opinion that counts

You will develop views about the market. Most of them will be wrong, and the
market has no obligation to inform you of this politely.

The only feedback that matters is the account balance over a large sample. Not
your reasoning, not how right you felt, not whether the move eventually went your
way after you were stopped out. A trade that was stopped out and then reversed
was still a correct trade if you followed your rules — the market simply took the
other side of a good bet, which it will do roughly half the time forever.

Hold that distinction and you can survive being wrong. Lose it and every loss
becomes evidence that you need to change something, which is how people churn
through nine strategies in a year and never accumulate the sample size to
evaluate any of them.

---

## The honest summary

You are attempting something with a documented sub-1% success rate, against
opponents with better information and better technology, using a skill that takes
years to develop and that most people never develop at all.

That is not a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to be serious about it: small
size, one instrument, written rules, complete records, income from elsewhere, and
a twelve-month horizon before you draw any conclusions.

Do it that way and the worst realistic outcome is that you spend a year, lose a
manageable amount of money, learn a great deal about markets and about yourself,
and stop. That is a genuinely fine outcome and it is far better than what happens
to most people who start.

Do it the other way — big size, forty tickers, no records, rent money, and a
three-month horizon — and you already know how that ends. Everybody does. It is
just that almost nobody believes it applies to them.
