# 08 — Red Flags

Day trading has the unusual property that **the most reliable way to profit from
it is to sell education about it.** Trading income is volatile and hard-won;
course income is recurring and requires no edge whatsoever.

Understanding this incentive structure protects you better than any specific
warning list. Once you see it, most of what follows is obvious.

---

## 1. The economics of trading education

Consider two business models:

**Trading:** volatile income, requires genuine and rare skill, capital at risk,
returns bounded by market opportunity and capital, no leverage from scale.

**Selling trading education:** recurring revenue, requires only marketing skill,
no capital at risk, unbounded scale, and — critically — **your customers'
failures do not affect your revenue.** They are attributed to their lack of
discipline.

The second is strictly better as a business. So a rational actor who could do
either would choose the second. Which means: **the fact that someone is selling
you trading education is weak evidence that they cannot trade profitably.** Not
proof — some genuinely skilled traders teach — but the prior should be skeptical,
not credulous.

Apply this test: *if their edge were real and scalable, why do they need your
$97/month?* A trader with a genuine, capital-scalable edge earns more by
deploying capital than by selling courses, and would be diluting their own edge
by publicizing it. Coherent answers exist (teaching is enjoyable; their strategy
is capacity-constrained; they are diversifying income). Note whether you get one.

## 2. Specific red flags

### In marketing

🚩 **Lifestyle imagery.** Rented Lamborghinis, private jets, ocean views. Trading
profits are numbers in an account, not props. The imagery sells a feeling
precisely because the returns cannot be sold on their merits.

🚩 **Screenshots of profits.** Trivially faked, and even when genuine, show only
selected days. Nobody screenshots the losing weeks. Meaningful evidence would be
a multi-year audited track record — which is essentially never offered.

🚩 **"I turned $500 into $50,000."** Either false, survivorship bias (the hundred
who tried the same thing and lost are not making videos), or achieved with risk
that will eventually produce the reverse.

🚩 **Urgency and scarcity.** "Doors close at midnight." "Only 10 spots." These
are conversion tactics designed to prevent you from researching. A legitimate
educational product does not degrade if you think about it for a week.

🚩 **Guaranteed returns.** No legitimate market participant guarantees returns.
In many jurisdictions, doing so is itself a securities violation. This one is not
a yellow flag; it is disqualifying.

🚩 **"Secret" or "proprietary" methods.** The genuinely valuable methods in
finance are quantitative, capital-intensive, and never sold retail. What is sold
as secret is invariably a repackaging of publicly available technical analysis.

### In the product

🚩 **Signal services.** Someone tells you what to trade for a fee. Even where
signals are honest, you learn nothing, cannot evaluate the edge, and cannot
continue without paying. Where they are not honest, the service front-runs its
own subscribers.

🚩 **Chatrooms with a "call-out" leader.** Structurally similar to a
pump-and-dump: the leader enters, announces, subscribers buy and push price up,
the leader exits into that liquidity. This has been prosecuted repeatedly. The
subscribers provide the exit.

🚩 **Affiliate-linked broker recommendations** without disclosure. The
recommendation may be driven by commission rather than execution quality.

🚩 **Upsells.** The $47 course leads to the $497 course leads to the $4,997
mentorship. The funnel is the business.

### In social media

🚩 **Verified-looking accounts DMing you.** Universally a scam.

🚩 **Anyone offering to trade on your behalf** for a share of profits, absent
registration as an investment adviser. This is both a scam pattern and typically
illegal.

🚩 **Crypto or forex "account managers."** Among the most common fraud vectors
online. The pattern: small initial "profits" shown on a fake dashboard to induce
larger deposits, then withdrawal becomes impossible pending a "tax" or "fee"
payment — which is itself the second stage of the theft.

🚩 **Romance or friendship followed by an investment opportunity.** The
"pig butchering" pattern. Deliberately slow, emotionally involved, and
financially devastating. The relationship exists to make the investment
plausible.

## 3. What legitimate education looks like

It exists. It has these characteristics:

✅ **Teaches risk management before strategy.** The ordering is diagnostic.
✅ **States realistic base rates**, including that most traders lose.
✅ **Shows losses**, and discusses them without embarrassment.
✅ **Explains reasoning** rather than issuing signals.
✅ **Makes no income promises.**
✅ **Does not manufacture urgency.**
✅ **Discloses conflicts of interest.**
✅ **Encourages paper trading first**, even though this delays your purchase.
✅ **Is often free or cheap** — books, academic papers, exchange and regulator
   materials.

**Genuinely good free resources:**

- **Academic research** — the papers cited in the README are more informative
  about your actual odds than any course.
- **Regulator materials** — [SEC Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/),
  [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors), [CFTC](https://www.cftc.gov/) publish
  clear, unbiased educational content and fraud advisories.
- **Exchange education** — CME Group and others publish solid technical material
  on their own products.
- **Books** — *Trading in the Zone* (Douglas) on psychology; *Reminiscences of a
  Stock Operator* (Lefèvre) for the timeless behavioral material; *Market
  Wizards* (Schwager) for range, read critically for survivorship bias.
- **Your broker's paper trading platform** — free, and more valuable than any
  paid course.

## 4. The verification checklist

Before paying anyone anything:

- [ ] Search their name plus "scam," "review," "lawsuit," "SEC," "FINRA."
- [ ] Check [FINRA BrokerCheck](https://brokercheck.finra.org/) and
      [SEC IAPD](https://adviserinfo.sec.gov/) for registration and disciplinary
      history.
- [ ] Ask for an **audited** track record. Note the response — the deflection
      itself is the answer.
- [ ] Ask what percentage of students become profitable. Legitimate educators
      either know or acknowledge they do not track it.
- [ ] Check whether "testimonials" are paid, or the same faces across multiple
      unrelated products.
- [ ] Confirm a refund policy exists and read its actual conditions.
- [ ] Ask yourself the core question: *if their edge is real, why do they need my
      money?*

## 5. On regulated prop firms and funded accounts

A specific category worth its own note, since it has grown quickly.

Some are legitimate. Many operate on a structure where **the evaluation fee is
the revenue**, and the pass rate is low by design. Warning signs:

- The firm will not disclose its pass rate.
- Rules are numerous and strict enough that failure on a technicality is likely
  (trailing drawdown measured on unrealized equity is the classic).
- "Payouts" come from firm capital rather than real market positions, meaning you
  are in a simulated environment and the firm's interest is in your failure.
- Marketing emphasizes the size of the funded account rather than the terms of
  the arrangement.

Ask directly: what percentage of participants receive a payout? A firm that
declines to answer has answered.

## 6. If you have been scammed

It happens to intelligent, careful people. The schemes are professionally
designed and specifically engineered to defeat ordinary skepticism. Being
defrauded is not a character failing.

**Act immediately — speed materially affects recovery odds:**

1. **Stop all payments.** Send nothing further. Any demand for an additional fee
   to "release" your funds is a continuation of the fraud, without exception.
2. **Contact your bank or card issuer** about a chargeback or recall. Some
   transfers can be reversed within a short window.
3. **Document everything** — screenshots, messages, transaction records, URLs,
   account names, before they are deleted.
4. **Report it:**
   - SEC: [sec.gov/tcr](https://www.sec.gov/tcr)
   - CFTC: [cftc.gov/complaint](https://www.cftc.gov/complaint)
   - FBI IC3: [ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov/)
   - FTC: [reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/)
   - Your state securities regulator
5. **Beware recovery scams.** Anyone who contacts you offering to recover lost
   funds for an upfront fee is targeting you a second time, often using victim
   lists from the first fraud. Legitimate recovery goes through regulators and
   law enforcement, not inbound DMs.

---

## What to take from this chapter

1. Selling trading education is a better business than trading. Weight that.
2. Lifestyle marketing, urgency, guaranteed returns, and "secret" methods are
   reliable negative signals.
3. Signal services and call-out chatrooms are structurally adverse to you.
4. Legitimate education teaches risk first and states real base rates.
5. Verify registration before paying anyone.
6. If defrauded: stop payments, document, report fast, and ignore recovery
   offers.
